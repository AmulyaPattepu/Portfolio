Standing: designing for the silence after you press Apply

An unofficial concept redesign of what happens after a candidate presses Apply on LinkedIn. Every application ends in a clear status, and a recruiter can give that answer to everyone still waiting in one step.

The problem

Candidates can handle a no. What they can't handle is not knowing.

Jordan has sent 48 applications this month. Most of his list still says "Applied", long after the real answer was no. He keeps a spreadsheet just to guess which jobs are still alive.

11,000applications sent on LinkedIn every minute in 2025, up 45% in a year
93%of US job seekers in one survey had applied and never heard back
61%of job seekers had been ghosted after an interview

Sources: The New York Times via eWeek, a NumberBarn survey via Allwork.Space, and Greenhouse.

Recruiters are drowning too: roles now get more than 300 applicants on average (HR Dive). LinkedIn already has some of the pieces, but they're thin and optional. Hiring systems can send it only three events, and only if the employer opts in. A rejection arrives by email but, per LinkedIn's own developer documentation, doesn't appear in the candidate's list of applications.

How might every application end in an honest answer, without asking an overloaded recruiter to write 200 emails?

Research

What I looked at

  • Desk research: application volumes, ghosting surveys from Greenhouse, iHire and NumberBarn, and LinkedIn's developer documentation on which statuses employers can send.
  • Competitor review: Indeed's application statuses, Greenhouse's MyGreenhouse portal, and the trackers Teal and Huntr.
  • Candidate and recruiter stories: public posts and comments on Reddit, LinkedIn and Instagram, organised into two candidate and two recruiter composite profiles.

Composite profiles show patterns across many people, but they are not interviews. I treated every insight as a hypothesis to check in usability testing.

The most useful find: Indeed's "Response seems unlikely" proves a platform can end the silence itself, without waiting for an employer.

Insights

Recruiters weren't against answering. The tools only offered a final no.

  1. The missing status is "still being considered". Recruiters hold candidates while a hiring manager decides, and won't reject anyone too early. They need an honest middle state.
  2. A promised date is where trust breaks. Both candidates' worst silence came after "we'll get back to you within a week".
  3. Reply times are welcome if they're measured, not promised. Both recruiters would show a typical range from their own history, like "7–10 days".
  4. One follow-up is fair on both sides. Candidates send one and stop, afraid of being a bother; recruiters accept one, but not repeated messages.
  5. Half of all applications happen off LinkedIn. Both candidates found jobs there but applied on company sites, then tracked them in a spreadsheet, their inbox or their head.
  6. People already decide when an application is dead, after two to four weeks of silence. The product can say it for them, kindly.

The project started as a tracker redesign for candidates. The research turned it into a two-sided problem: make every outcome cheap to give and honest to receive.

Principles and scope

Four rules that decided what got built

  1. Every application ends in an answer. Silence becomes a status: a measured reply window, then "likely closed" when it passes.
  2. Measured, never promised. Reply times are ranges from an employer's own history. Only a recruiter sets a date.
  3. A no is information, not an error. Rejection appears in calm neutral ink. There's no red anywhere in the system.
  4. One step to be kind. Every recruiter action that closes a loop is a single step, for one person or 140.
In scope
  • Applications grouped into In progress, Waiting and Closed
  • A reply window measured from the employer's history
  • "Still being considered", with a date the recruiter sets
  • "Ask for an update", once per stage
  • Clear outcomes and "likely closed" on the application itself
  • Adding an outside application
  • "Close the loop" for many applicants at once
Left out, on purpose
  • Screening and ranking candidates
  • AI matching and resume tools
  • Messaging
  • How hiring systems connect to LinkedIn

Each is a different problem, and the concept stays believable without them.

User flows

Nobody is rejected unless a person chooses to

The platform only fills the silence with what it can measure. People make every decision.

Flow 1 · Jordan, applying to a clear outcome
  1. Applies on LinkedIn or a company site
  2. Listed as Waiting, with a measured reply window
  3. Update inside the window?If not, he can ask once; if it's still silent, it's marked likely closed
  4. The timeline moves: viewed, in review, interviewing
  5. Still being considered, with a decision date
  6. Decided on time?If not, the recruiter is reminded and Jordan can ask once
  7. A clear outcome: offer, not moving forward, or filled
Flow 2 · Helen, shortlist to closing the loop
  1. Reviews applicants on LinkedIn or her hiring system
  2. Builds a shortlist and interviews the top few
  3. Has the manager decided?If not, she sets a decision date, shown as "still being considered"
  4. Selects everyone not moving on, often 100 or more
  5. Chooses "Close the loop" and edits the message, or not
  6. Previews exactly what candidates will see, then sends
  7. Every application updates with a clear outcome

The "still being considered" branch is what makes Helen willing to answer early: she can be honest without closing a door the hiring manager hasn't closed yet.

Wireframes

Nine screens in grey, then five changes

Seven candidate screens on mobile and two recruiter screens on the web, to test the order of information before any colour.

A progress bar became a reply windowThe wireframe filled a bar toward day 10. The final design shades the employer's usual range on a 14-day track, so "day 6" reads as "not late yet", not "60% done".
A date in a sentence became a calendar tile"Decision expected by Fri 9 Oct" was easy to skim past. An amber tile makes the one date a recruiter committed to impossible to miss.
"No reply" became "Likely closed", with the reasonThe card says how long it's been and how long this employer usually takes, so the label reads as a measurement, not a verdict.
The update request explains itselfThe sheet shows who will receive it and why it's available now: the decision date has passed.

The fifth change: recruiters see what candidates will see. The "Close the loop" dialog shows the exact status chip and date candidates will get, not a sentence describing it.

The solution

One honest list, and one step to answer everyone

Standing turns a long list of "Applied" into three honest groups, and gives recruiters a single step to answer everyone waiting.

Design system

A small system, also called Standing, built around time: a paper-white page, near-black ink and one plum accent. Each colour has one job.

What I changed after testing

  • Every application says what to do now. The candidate asked for an obvious "what should I do?" on each application. Each one now opens with a single next step: wait, follow up, stop waiting or prepare, with the date that changes it.
  • The reply window got a legend. The shaded bar took a few seconds to read. A legend under it now names both parts: the usual reply time over the last 90 days, and today.
  • Nothing is sent without a review. The recruiter wasn't sure whether the message would go out automatically. The selection bar and the dialog now say nothing is sent until she presses Send.
  • Messages greet each person by name. She wanted it more personal without editing 140 messages, so the message starts with "Hi {first name}".
  • "Position on hold" became a fourth answer. She asked for more ready-made statuses. "Still reviewing" was already covered by "Still being considered".

Two participants is a small sample, so I read these as signals rather than proof. Both came from my own network, so the next round should include candidates outside tech and recruiters at larger companies.

Reflection

The most valuable decision was adding a middle, not more endings

  • Honesty needs a middle. Recruiters stayed silent because the only answer available was a final no. "Still being considered", with a date, lets them be honest early.
  • Measure, don't promise. Showing an employer's real reply time is fairer than any promise, and it quietly rewards the employers who reply.
  • Redesign inside the constraints. Standing reuses what LinkedIn already has (a tracker, status events, rejection tools) and makes it visible and cheap to use. That's what makes it believable.

Next: test with candidates outside tech and recruiters at larger companies, then design the recruiter's one-tap reply to an update request and a weekly summary notification.
