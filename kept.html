Kept: designing for the busiest day of a small seller's week

A dashboard that turns WhatsApp, Instagram and Facebook chats into confirmed orders, built around the moment a post goes viral and the phone won't stop buzzing.

The problem

Orders that arrive as conversations

A clothing reseller posts 15 pieces on Instagram. Within an hour, messages pour in across Instagram, WhatsApp and Facebook: "Is the navy one available?", "M or L?", "Send it to the address near the park." She replies to each, copies the details into a spreadsheet, checks her bank to see who has really paid, and forwards screenshots to whoever is helping her pack.

Tools built for online stores assume a website, a catalogue and a checkout. These sellers have none of that. Existing tools either live inside one app, assume a Shopify store, or track stock without ever seeing the chats.

How might we help a small seller stay in control of orders, stock and payments on the day her post goes viral, without changing how her customers like to buy?

Research

What I looked at

  • Desk research: reports on social and conversational commerce, guides written for social sellers, and data on reply times, overselling and payment fraud.
  • Competitor review: six tools across chat-to-order apps, WhatsApp inboxes and CRMs, and multichannel inventory software.
  • Seller profiles: app reviews, articles and seller discussions organised into five composite profiles: a home baker, a clothing reseller, a snacks seller, a jewellery maker and an accessories seller.

Composite profiles show patterns across many sellers, but they are not interviews. I treated every insight as a hypothesis and checked it with a usability test.

Insights

Sellers weren't struggling to make or pack. They were struggling to remember.

  1. Tracking is the bottleneck. Sellers can handle 30–40 orders; what breaks is finding one buyer's size in 100+ messages.
  2. Approval is non-negotiable. Help drafting orders was welcome, but only if the seller approves each one. Nothing may message buyers automatically.
  3. Payment checking is already a habit. Sellers distrust screenshots and check their bank. They need a clear Paid / Unpaid status, not fraud detection.
  4. "Stock" means two things. Resellers run out of units; makers run out of time slots and materials.
  5. Helpers are cut off. Family members who pack work from forwarded screenshots, then come back with questions.
  6. Past tools added work. Shopify felt too formal, inventory software was built for shops, Trello needed manual updates.

These cut my first idea down: auto-replies, payment links and TikTok support all went.

Principles and scope

Four rules that decided what got built

  1. Calm in the rush. The opposite of a phone that won't stop buzzing.
  2. The seller approves, always. AI drafts; people decide.
  3. No double entry. If a seller types something twice, the design failed.
  4. Status in words, not just colour. Every state carries a label.
In scope
  • One inbox, one buyer's chats merged
  • AI draft orders the seller approves
  • Live stock in units or daily capacity
  • Paid / Unpaid after a bank check
  • Order board from Unpaid to Delivered
  • Mobile pick list for helpers
  • Address check with a location pin request
Left out, on purpose
  • Automatic replies to buyers (breaks the approval rule)
  • Fake-screenshot detection (sellers already check their bank)
  • TikTok (never came up in research)
  • Payment and courier integrations
User flows

Nothing becomes an order until the seller approves it

Flow 1 · chat to paid order
  1. A message arrives on WhatsApp, Instagram or Facebook
  2. AI drafts an order card: product, variant, quantity, address
  3. Are the details clear?If not, unclear fields are highlighted; she edits or asks the buyer
  4. Is it in stock?If not, a sold-out warning; she offers another option
  5. Priya approves; the order is saved as Unpaid and stock is held
  6. She checks her bank and marks it Paid
Flow 2 · paid order to delivered
  1. The paid order appears on Arjun's pick list
  2. Is the address complete?If not, Priya sends a one-tap location pin request
  3. Arjun packs it and taps Packed
  4. The board updates live: Packed, then Delivered

Validation

Three sellers, four tasks, zero oversold items

I tested the prototype with three people who sell through chat apps: a home baker, a clothing reseller and a homemade snacks seller. Each tried four tasks: approve a draft with an unclear size, check whether the last L could be promised, decide whether a payment screenshot means Paid, and pack the next order as the helper.

Task success
3 of 3 completed all four tasks without help
Time to approve an order
15–30 sec vs. 2–4 min by hand
Sold-out items promised
0, all three spotted that L was sold out
Payment screenshot
3 of 3 checked their bank before marking Paid
Average ease rating
4.8 / 5

"I liked that it pulled the information from the chat instead of making me type everything again."

What I changed after testing

  • Payment status is now unmissable. A large Not paid yet / Paid block replaced the small chip, after one seller asked for "a big Paid/Not Paid indicator".
  • The address moved up. It now sits right under the customer's name in bolder type, because sellers always double-check it.
  • Every order shows what's left. A tracker runs Approved → Paid → Packed → Delivered, with the next step in plain words.
  • A short "Start here" tip on the first screen, since one seller found it busy until she understood the layout.

Three participants is a small sample, so I read these as strong signals rather than proof. Every participant said they would trust AI drafts only if they approve each one, which confirms the core principle.

Reflection

The most valuable decision was what I took out

  • Restraint beat features. Auto-replies and payment links looked impressive but clashed with how sellers want to work.
  • AI needs a visible boundary. Its own colour and a clear draft-to-kept moment answer "can I trust this?"
  • Design for the worst day. Designing for the rush sharpened every decision, from the sale mode bar to the pick list.

Next: custom draft fields for makers, like delivery date and flavour (the baker checked these most), then onboarding: connecting channels and importing products from photos.
