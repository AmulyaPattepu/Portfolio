Onward: designing for the worst ten minutes of a trip

A mobile app that tells a stranded traveler what to do right now, what to ask the airline for, and what they're owed, built around the moment a flight is cancelled at the gate.

The problem

The hard part isn't the wait. It's not knowing what to do.

Maya's 18:40 flight from Paris to Rome is cancelled at the gate. 180 people head for one desk. The airline app hasn't updated yet, she doesn't know her options, and she has no idea whether she's owed anything.

1 in 9US flights arrived 45+ minutes late in 2025, the worst since 2014
15%of disrupted travelers sorted it out within 30 minutes
42%paid costs that were never reimbursed

Sources: U.S. PIRG, and a survey of 1,000+ US travelers by Hopper Technology Solutions reported by Fortune.

The rules make it harder. Europe and the UK pay cash for long delays and cancellations, the US guarantees refunds but not compensation, and India pays only for late-notice cancellations. Most people don't know which applies to them.

How might we get a stranded traveler from "my flight is cancelled" to rebooked, looked after and ready to claim, in the ten minutes before the queue decides for them?

Research

What I looked at

  • Desk research: delay data for the US and Europe, a large traveler survey, and passenger rules for the EU, UK, US and India, including the EU reform approved in July 2026.
  • Competitor review: airline apps, Flighty, TripIt Pro and claim companies like AirHelp, mapped to the moment of a disruption each one covers.
  • Traveler profiles: public reviews, complaints and social media threads organised into three composite profiles: Manchester to Barcelona, Bengaluru to Kozhikode, and New York to Milan.

Composite profiles show patterns across many travelers, but they are not interviews. I treated every insight as a hypothesis to check in usability testing.

The gap: alerts tell you something went wrong, airline apps only help with their own flights, and claim companies arrive weeks later and keep 35% of the payout. Nobody answers "what do I do right now, and what am I owed?" at the gate.

Insights

Travelers didn't need more alerts. They needed a next step.

  1. Everyone self-serves first. All three opened an app or a search within minutes. The desk queue was a backup, not the plan.
  2. The answer differs by journey. One traveler was probably owed £220 and never claimed; another wasn't covered by EU rules at all. "You're owed" and "you're not" need equal clarity.
  3. The biggest fear is spending your own money. One traveler paid $780 for a new ticket without knowing whether the airline would cover it.
  4. Small costs get abandoned. £25–30 and ₹3,000 were written off, because there was no receipt trail.
  5. The flight is only part of it. Hotels, travel companions and plans at the destination all need rearranging.

The project started as a flight booking app. The research moved it to the moment booking apps leave you alone.

Principles and scope

Four rules that decided what got built

  1. One next step, always. Every screen ends in a single yellow bar that says what to do now.
  2. Prepare, don't pretend. A third-party app can't rebook seats or file claims, so Onward prepares everything and hands off to the airline.
  3. Plain words, honest answers. "You may be owed €250", with the reason and the official rule. "Nothing is owed" is said just as clearly.
  4. Built for a shaking hand. Big type, high contrast, 48px targets and one-thumb reach.
In scope
  • Lock-screen alert with one next step
  • Do-this-now plan
  • Options side by side
  • "What you may be owed": EU and US designed, UK and India rules researched
  • "Ask the desk for" checklist and a card for the agent
  • Receipt log
  • Free claim pack the traveler sends
Left out, on purpose
  • Booking seats directly (needs airline partnerships)
  • Flight search and fares
  • Payments
  • Filing claims or legal advice for the traveler
User flows

Onward prepares; the airline's own channels do the rest

Flow 1 · cancellation to updated trip
  1. Cancellation alert on the lock screen, with one next step
  2. The disruption plan opens: rebook, queue, call
  3. Options side by side: next flights, refund, another route
  4. Rebook or refund?A refund is requested in the airline app and tracked here
  5. She rebooks in the airline app through a deep link
  6. Overnight wait?If so, the checklist says to ask for a hotel and meals
  7. She logs any receipts for what she pays herself
  8. Trip updated; the claim pack starts filling
Flow 2 · end of trip to sent claim
  1. The trip ends; flight, delay and cause are saved
  2. "What you're owed" checks the rule for this exact journey
  3. Likely owed?If not, a clear no with the reason, plus refund and care rights
  4. The claim pack is prefilled with the flight, delay and receipts
  5. She reviews it and sends it through the airline's own form
  6. A reminder follows up if the airline hasn't replied
Wireframes

Seven screens in grey, then five changes

I tested the order of information in low fidelity first, from the lock-screen alert to the claim pack.

A tab bar became a trackerFour tabs suggested four separate places. A disruption is a sequence, so a three-step tracker (Rebook, Looked after, Owed) shows where you are.
Many buttons became one barEach card had its own button. Now one yellow next-step bar sits at the bottom of every screen, within thumb reach.
A wrong label was fixedThe wireframe called the next-morning flight "Fastest", but the Milan route lands earlier. It's now "Earliest", and every option shows how much later it lands.
The agent card got its own screenA plain sentence for the desk agent, in large type on a dark background, so Maya can simply turn her phone around.

The fifth change: a clear no got its own screen, so Dana's New York flight gets an answer too.

The solution

Five moments, one next step each

The final UI follows Maya from the alert to the claim. The flight card borrows the look of a departures board, so the most important facts read at a glance.

Design system

A small system, also called Onward, so every screen reads like airport signage: calm, high-contrast and instantly scannable. Each colour has one job.

Atkinson Hyperlegible was designed by the Braille Institute for low-vision readers. Its monospaced sibling lines numbers up and keeps 0 from looking like O. Status always carries a word, and every target is at least 48px.

Validation

I tested the prototype with two travelers who have had a flight cancelled: a UK traveler on a phone and an Italian traveler on a laptop. What I changed after testing

  • "Likely owed" became "may be owed". One traveler paused on €250, unsure whether it was guaranteed or an estimate. "Not guaranteed" and the reason now sit directly under the amount.
  • The claim pack says what happens next. One traveler wasn't sure whether to download it now or finish later. It now says "Saved in Onward. Nothing to download." and shows three numbered steps to send it.

Two participants is a small sample, so I read these as signals rather than proof. One traveler chose the direct flight over the earlier one, which supports showing options side by side instead of ranking one as best. Dana's "nothing owed" screen wasn't tested in this round; it's first in line for the next one.

Reflection

The most valuable decision was one next step per screen

  • Stress changes what good design means. On a calm day, choice is a feature. At a cancelled gate, it's a burden.
  • Honesty is a feature. Testing showed that even "likely owed" read like a promise. "May be owed", with "Not guaranteed" right under it, is less exciting and more trustworthy.
  • Knowing the limits kept the scope real. Hand-offs to the airline's own channels make Onward buildable without partnerships.

Next: test Dana's "nothing owed" screen with more travelers, then design a day-before heads-up for when the inbound plane is already late, and a way to add a trip from a booking email.
